Every rupee of scalper markup is money the artist, the venue and the promoter created and did not capture — and every fake ticket is a fan who will not come back. ReXell makes tickets un-scalpable by binding them to a person, then sells organizers the secondary market they never controlled.

The problem. Live events lose money and goodwill at both ends of the ticket. Counterfeits and duplicated barcodes turn up at the gate; bots and scalpers buy inventory at face value and resell it at multiples, capturing margin that the organizer, the artist and the venue generated. The organizer sees none of that uplift, absorbs the support cost, and takes the reputational damage when a fan is turned away holding a ticket they paid three times over for.

The product. ReXell issues each ticket as a blockchain-recorded entitlement bound to the buyer's biometric identity. There is no barcode to copy, no PDF to forward and no screenshot to sell, because the credential is the fan's face. Organizers switch resale on or off per ticket tier; when it is on, they set a price ceiling, a window and a commission, and a smart contract splits every resale automatically. An AI layer scores every purchase request in under 50 milliseconds to keep bot farms out of the queue.

The business. Three revenue lines on the same transaction: a per-ticket service fee from the fan, a platform fee on primary gross value from the organizer, and a commission on every resale — the last of which is revenue that does not exist for the organizer today. At a mid-size festival that is roughly ₹14.4 lakh of revenue on ₹2.2 crore of gross ticket value, at an 81% contribution margin once field operations are self-serve.

Why now. Three curves are crossing: biometric verification has become cheap and accurate enough to run on a mid-range Android phone; regulators in multiple markets are actively hostile to bot-driven resale; and live-event attendance in India, Southeast Asia and the Gulf is growing far faster than the ticketing infrastructure serving it. The incumbents in those markets compete on catalogue and discovery, not on trust at the gate.

The ask. $750,000 pre-seed for 18 months of runway, to reach 60 events, $12M cumulative gross ticket value, and three anchor organizer contracts — with the technical co-founder hire as the first and most important use of the money.

How to read the numbers in this plan

Market sizes marked with a tilde are planning estimates built from public industry reporting and bottom-up event maths, not commissioned research. The unit economics are modelled from a specified event and every input is shown so it can be argued with. Before this plan goes to a term sheet, the market figures should be replaced with sourced, dated citations and the per-event model validated against two real pilot events.

01Problem

The problem and the leak

Ticketing has an unusual failure: the product works, the money leaks. A sold-out show is a commercial success on paper while a large share of the value it created is harvested by parties who contributed nothing to it. Follow one ticket.

2.1Why the wedge is the wedge

India is the right first market for reasons that have nothing to do with the founder being here. Gate fraud at large domestic events is normalised rather than exceptional; the biggest ticketing platforms are discovery businesses with weak entry infrastructure; smartphone penetration and Android-first assumptions match the technology exactly; and biometric identity is culturally and legally established at national scale, which means face verification at a gate is unremarkable rather than alarming to a mainstream fan.

The counterweight is honest: average ticket prices are low, so revenue per event is lower than in Europe or North America, and the business only works at volume. That is precisely why the plan front-loads events over average deal size, and why the second market is the Gulf, where average ticket values are three to five times higher for a similar operational effort.

03Product

Product and what is defensible

Most of what ReXell does exists somewhere already. Rotating barcodes exist. Capped resale exists. Biometric stadium entry exists. NFT tickets exist. None of the players doing one of those things does the combination, and the combination is what changes the economics.

CapabilityWhat it does for the customerHow defensible, honestly
Face as the ticketNothing to copy, forward, screenshot or forge. Entry in under a second. The person who bought it is the person who gets in.low The matching technology is licensable by anyone. The defensibility is in the operational track record of running it at a real gate without a queue collapse.
Organizer-programmable resaleA switch and three dials: on or off, price ceiling, window, commission. Enforced in the contract, not in the app.medium Easy to copy technically, hard to copy commercially — it requires organizer trust and a settlement relationship.
Automated commission splitResale revenue arrives without an invoice, a reconciliation call or a trust assumption. Independently verifiable.medium The audit trail is a genuine sales asset when negotiating with a promoter who has been burned by a settlement dispute.
Bot defense at onsaleA queue that honest fans can actually win, and bulk acquisition that costs the scalper real money.high, with time Detection improves with labelled outcomes, and only ReXell has the gate-denial and dedupe labels. This is the compounding moat.
Verified attendance dataSponsors and organizers learn who actually turned up, not who bought.high A dataset no competitor without identity binding can produce, and the highest-margin revenue line in the model.

The defensibility argument in one sentence

The technology is copyable and the moat is the data flywheel it produces: every gate denial, every dedupe link and every chargeback is a label that no competitor without identity binding can generate, and those labels are what make the bot model better each month. The plan should be defended on that basis, not on the biometrics.

04Model

Revenue model and pricing

Five lines, three of which bill on the same transaction. The pricing deliberately keeps the organizer's marginal cost near zero at signup — the platform fee is small and the resale commission is money they do not currently have — so the sales conversation is about upside rather than budget.

07Go-to-market

Go-to-market

Ticketing is a supply-constrained market: fans go where the tickets are, so the only side worth selling to first is the organizer. That makes this a founder-led enterprise sale with a small number of large, slow, high-value decisions — and the founder's existing sponsorship track record is the relevant credential.

7.1Three phases

Prove

7.2The sales motion

  • Lead with the resale commission, not the biometrics. A promoter's first question about face scanning is a risk question. Their first question about a new revenue line is a maths question. Open with the maths.
  • Use the artist as the lever. Artists hate being blamed for scalped prices. An artist or their management asking a promoter to use ReXell converts faster than any outbound sequence, and costs nothing.
  • Sell the guarantee, and mean it. A financially backed zero-fake-ticket guarantee is a differentiator no incumbent can match without identity binding, and the expected payout is low precisely because the system works. Underwrite it explicitly and put the reserve in the model.
  • Land on one tier, not the whole event. A promoter will not put 12,000 tickets through an unproven platform. They will put the 800-ticket VIP tier through it, where fraud hurts most and the gate is already staffed.
  • Sponsors fund the pilot. The founder has already sold $50k of sponsorship pre-product. Sponsor money that pays for a pilot event's field ops turns the riskiest, lowest-margin phase into a self-funding one.

09Regulatory

Regulatory reality

Biometrics plus payments plus a blockchain is three regulated surfaces on one product. None of it is prohibitive; all of it is expensive to get wrong, and two items below are genuine go or no-go decisions for specific geographies.

RegimeObligationPosition
India DPDP Act, 2023Itemised notice, purpose limitation, withdrawable consent, breach notification, named data protection officerDesigned in: unbundled biometric consent, versioned consent records, verified deletion. Budget for a fractional privacy counsel from month one.
GDPR Article 9Explicit consent for biometric identification, completed DPIA, data-residency careRequired before any EU event. Treat as a phase-three cost, not a launch cost.
Illinois BIPA and similarWritten release, published retention schedule, statutory damages per violation, no profiting from biometric dataGo / no-go. The class-action exposure is disproportionate to any early revenue. Recommendation: geofence biometric enrolment out of those states and run the non-biometric path there.
India virtual digital asset tax30% tax on VDA gains plus 1% TDS on transfersGo / no-go, resolved by design. Tickets are never traded as crypto assets and fans never hold tokens; all settlement is in rupees through a licensed aggregator. Requires a written tax opinion before the first resale goes live.
Payments and settlementPayment-aggregator licensing, escrow and settlement timelines, GST on service feesPartner with a licensed aggregator rather than seeking a licence. ReXell never holds fan money on its own balance sheet.
Consumer and resale lawDisclosure of fees, resale caps in several jurisdictions, US BOTS ActTailwind rather than headwind — regulators are already pushing where ReXell is going. Worth using in sales conversations.
Accessibility and non-discriminationEqual access for fans who cannot or will not enrol biometricallyThe non-biometric entry path is a product requirement, not a concession, and must never carry a fee or a worse queue.
